A fungus is any member of the group of eukaryotic organisms that includes microorganisms such as yeasts and molds, as well as the more familiar mushrooms. These organisms are classified as a kingdom, separately from plants, animals, and protists.

Fungi play crucial roles in the energy cycle of the biosphere, and in the decomposition of organic matter. They convert hard-to-digest polysaccharides into simpler molecules which can be used by other organisms. Many fungi live in symbiotic relationships with plants, helping them absorb nutrients from the soil.

Fungi have long been used as a direct source of human food. Mushrooms, morels, and truffles are all edible fungi that have been eaten by humans for thousands of years. Fungi are also used in fermentation processes to produce bread, wine, beer, and cheese. The study of fungi is known as mycology.

The word fungus is the Latin word for mushroom. Fungi are found in most ecosystems, including marine, freshwater, and terrestrial habitats. They grow in moist environments where organic material is present. Many fungal species produce fruiting bodies that are visible to the naked eye, such as mushrooms and puffballs.

Fungi reproduce through spores, which can be sexual or asexual. These spores are often distributed by wind, water, or animals. When a spore lands in a suitable environment, it germinates and forms a new fungal organism. The main body of a fungus consists of a network of thread-like structures called hyphae, which collectively form a mycelium.

Fungi obtain nutrients through absorption rather than photosynthesis or ingestion. They secrete enzymes that break down organic matter externally, then absorb the resulting molecules. This process makes fungi excellent decomposers in ecosystems. Without fungi, dead plant and animal matter would accumulate, and nutrients would not be recycled back into the soil.

Some fungi are parasitic, obtaining nutrients from living organisms and potentially causing disease. Plant pathogenic fungi cause many crop diseases, resulting in significant economic losses. Examples include rusts, smuts, and mildews. However, many other fungi form beneficial partnerships with plants through mycorrhizal associations, where the fungus helps the plant absorb water and minerals while receiving carbohydrates in return.

Fungi have been used in traditional medicine for centuries, and modern science has validated many of these uses. The antibiotic penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming, is produced by the Penicillium fungus. Other medically important compounds derived from fungi include immunosuppressants, cholesterol-lowering drugs, and anticancer agents.

In forest ecosystems, fungi play an essential role in decomposing wood and leaf litter. Many forest fungi form mycorrhizal associations with trees, creating vast underground networks that connect multiple plants. These networks, sometimes called the "wood wide web," allow trees to share nutrients and even communicate with each other through chemical signals.

The diversity of fungi is immense, with estimates suggesting there may be between 2.2 and 3.8 million fungal species on Earth. However, only about 150,000 species have been formally described. New fungal species are constantly being discovered, particularly in tropical regions and extreme environments.

Fungi have adapted to survive in nearly every environment on Earth, from arctic tundra to desert sands, and from deep ocean floors to the highest mountain peaks. Some fungi can even break down petroleum products, plastics, and other synthetic materials, making them valuable for bioremediation efforts.

Climate change poses both challenges and opportunities for fungal communities. As temperatures rise and precipitation patterns shift, the distribution and activity of fungi are changing. Understanding these changes is crucial for predicting ecosystem responses to climate change and managing forests and agricultural systems sustainably.
